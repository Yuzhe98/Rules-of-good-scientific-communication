\section{Introduction}

Excellent scientific communication demands excellent understanding of science in the first place, and then demands good communication skills.
% Scientific communications take forms of articles, colloquium, keynote speech, elevator chat, and etc. 
Previous education notes of the authors and the collaborators have covered communication media including articles\,\cite{Budker2008writing, Budker_Kimball_2016_collaborative}, posters\,\cite{Budker_Trabesinger_2017_Poster}, and viewgraphs\,\cite{Zhang_Budker_2024_viewgraphs}.
In this educational note, we present questions to help design good communication, along with advice for avoiding basic but unfortunately common mistakes.

\vspace{0.3\baselineskip}
\section{The guidelines}

Think of scientific communication as a journey. 
The communicator is the guide, and the audience are the travelers. 
The communicator and audience should share a destination (a goal), and the communicator should choose a route and pace the audience can follow.

To \textbf{establish the shared goal}, the communicator should ask:: 
\begin{center}
\textbf{What} is the topic, and \textbf{why} does it matter?
\end{center}
% The communicator and audience should share an understanding of the topic and why it matters.
To design the communication around this goal, the communicator should ask: 
\begin{center}
\textbf{Who} is the audience, and \textbf{where} and \textbf{when} does the communication happen?
\end{center}
% Good design begins with the audience and the situation. 
As one example, a quick survey before a colloquium can help the speaker understand the audience's background and expectations, and then adjust the content accordingly. 
% The setting, timing, and available communication media also shape the design.

With the common goal and audience in mind, the next question is:
\begin{center}
\textbf{How} does the communicator guide the audience?
\end{center}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{One thing at a time.} Do not challenge the concentration of the audience. 
    One common mistake, as an example, is introducing two topics within one viewgraph of a presentation.

    \item \textbf{Introduce every new element.} Here ``element'' includes theorems, abbreviations, adjectives, and often the communicator themself.
          The communicator's familiarity with these elements leads them to overestimate the audience's knowledge.
          Repeating definitions of new elements is good practice because it
          \begin{itemize}
              \item reinforces the audience's memory;
                    % \item make sure the audience is synchronized with you;
              \item slows down the flow of information, which is usually too fast and overwhelming;
              \item leaves some room for snoozing.
          \end{itemize}

          % \item \textbf{People are only smart with numbers smaller than 20.} Do not distract your audience by making them do math in heart. 

    \item \textbf{Example helps.} An example is worth a thousand sentences of explanation.


    \item \textbf{Highlights are made by contrast.} For example, relative magnitudes are favored over absolute ones when demonstrating improvements.

          % \item \textbf{Left to right / low to high corresponds naturally to small to large.} Better not to invert this relationship.

          % \item \textbf{Humans will naturally compare plots with the same $x$- and $y$-axis labels.}

    \item \textbf{Good answers are direct.} Who $\rightarrow$ someone; what $\rightarrow$ something; where $\rightarrow$ someplace; {when} $\rightarrow$ sometime; {why} $\rightarrow$ some reason; {how} $\rightarrow$ a description; yes/no question $\rightarrow$ yes/no.
          % Start your answer by a short and direct response. 

\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.3\baselineskip}

\section{Concluding remarks}

The guidelines presented can guarantee transparent communication.
Following them is not as easy as it seems. 
Artificial intelligence (AI) can help the communicator check how well their article or speech follows these guidelines.
